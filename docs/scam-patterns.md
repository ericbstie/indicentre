# What real scam messages look like

One-page note for writing IndiCentre's scam templates and victim replies (2026-11-02). Sources are public awareness pages: FTC romance data spotlight 2023, Police Scotland "Take Five" impersonation page, UK Finance alerts, Krebs on Security's 2018 sextortion write-up, Cofense on BEC gift-card scams, Avast and MalwareTips on marketplace fake buyers. Everything here is the awareness-level shape of the message, not how to run it.

## The general rule

Real scams aren't written well, and they aren't written badly by accident. Bulk scams (phishing, smishing, lottery, sextortion) are templates sent to thousands. They read like a form: reference numbers, boilerplate footer, a greeting that doesn't quite fit, a link that is one letter off. Targeted scams (BEC, romance, bank calls, marketplace) read like a busy person: short, lowercase, no signature or "Sent from my phone", one ask. Our current texts are too tidy in the middle. A real phishing mail is stiffer than ours, and a real CEO mail is sloppier.

## Victim replies

A win's reply lands in the scammer's inbox as "Re: ...", so it is the victim typing to the scammer, not talking to a reporter. Real replies are: short, thankful, a bit anxious, sometimes over-sharing a detail the scammer can use next ("my pension comes on the 20th"), sometimes asking the scammer for reassurance. Phone register: lowercase, missing commas, a typo now and then. The player should see the harm from what the victim says without the victim seeing it.

## What we keep

No real brands (Fjellstad Bank, QuickCrate, Lykke Sport, Thrivora). `{name}` only. No triads. Tells live inside the message, never explained after it. Nothing operational: no real domains, no working techniques beyond what awareness pages already publish.
